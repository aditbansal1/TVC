2.1 System overview (page 1)

Know cold
  • Flight computer: Teensy 4.1 = NXP i.MX RT1062, ARM Cortex-M7 at 600 MHz, 1 MB RAM. Runs Zephyr RTOS. Code in C++.
  • Why FlexCAN3: the RT1062 has three CAN controllers; only CAN3 supports CAN-FD. On the Teensy 4.1 it's on pins 30 (RX) and 31 (TX).
  • Actuators: BLDC motor directly driving a 4 mm lead screw. 1 rev = 4 mm, so 25.4 / 4 = 6.35 rev per inch.
  • Motor controllers: moteus r4.1 (open-source BLDC controller by mjbots). Does field-oriented control (FOC) and closes current, velocity and position loops internally at tens of kHz. You talk to it over CAN-FD with a register-based protocol.
  • Ground link: Ethernet, protobuf messages (nanopb on the Teensy). Python ground client with a live TVC dashboard and CSV logging.
Q: Walk me through what happens in one 20 ms cycle.
The kernel timer fires. The TVC thread copies the latest pitch/yaw command from the controller (under the mutex). It runs the supervisor's step on the replies from last cycle: checks for faults, advances the state machine, and decides what to send each axis (query, stop, position command, or set-home). It sends one frame per axis, every frame requesting a reply. It waits up to 5 ms for both replies, parses them, records latency, and publishes telemetry back to the controller. Then it blocks on the timer until the next period. CPU time is tens of microseconds; the rest is waiting.
Q: Why does the moteus close the position loop instead of your firmware?
The motor needs current and position control at kHz rates to be stable and stiff. I couldn't do that over a 50 Hz bus. The moteus does commutation and the tight loops locally; I send setpoints and limits. It's a standard split: fast inner loop near the hardware, slower outer loop in the flight computer. It also means a comms dropout doesn't leave the motor uncontrolled; it keeps holding its last command until its watchdog trips.
Q: Why 50 Hz? Isn't that slow for TVC?
50 Hz is the setpoint rate, not the actuator's control bandwidth; the moteus runs its loops far faster. The engine gimbal is a large mass moving at most 30°/s, so the mechanical bandwidth is low. 50 Hz with under 5 ms of latency was enough margin for the GNC rate we need. If GNC needed more, the bus has room: about 10% load now, and the 5 Mbps data phase would cut frame time by about 3×. Be careful not to claim a GNC bandwidth number you don't know.
Q: What exactly did you do versus your teammates?
Use the page-1 lists. Mine: CAN-FD bring-up, the moteus protocol and transport, the TVC thread, kinematics in firmware, the supervisor, bus debugging. Teammates: the controller framework, sensor drivers, the first moteus bench test (which set the controller IDs), and the gimbal mechanism and MATLAB model. Being precise here builds trust.

2.3 CAN-FD choice and physical layer (page 2)

Q: Why CAN-FD over classic CAN, RS-485, Ethernet, or PWM?
Classic CAN: 8 data bytes per frame; my 43-byte message would need 6 frames per axis, and moteus speaks CAN-FD anyway. RS-485/422: also differential and robust, but no built-in arbitration, error detection/retransmission or acknowledgement; I'd have to build a protocol. Ethernet: more bandwidth than needed, more complex, needs switches/magnetics, and it's not what the moteus supports. PWM: single-ended, one wire per actuator, no feedback, no error detection. CAN-FD gives a differential multi-drop bus, hardware CRC and ACK, automatic retransmission, and priority by ID.
Q: Why 120 Ω, and why at both ends?
A twisted pair has a characteristic impedance of roughly 120 Ω. When a signal edge travels down the line and hits an end whose impedance doesn't match, part of it reflects back. Terminating each end with a resistor equal to the line impedance absorbs the edge. Both ends, because the signal can travel either way: any node can transmit. The moteus boards have no termination on board, so I added it. Check: with power off, measure across CAN_H and CAN_L: two 120 Ω in parallel = 60 Ω. 120 Ω means one is missing; ~40 Ω means there's an extra one.
Q: What happens if you forget termination?
Reflections cause ringing on the edges. At low bit rates on a short bench cable it may "work"; at higher rates or longer harnesses the ringing lands on the sample point and you get bit errors, error frames, and eventually nodes going error-passive or bus-off. Also, without any termination the recessive state isn't actively pulled together (the termination is what discharges the bus back to recessive), so edges get slow. This is exactly why you verify signal quality on the real harness before raising the data rate.
Q: What do CAN_H and CAN_L look like on a scope?
Recessive (logic 1): both about 2.5 V, differential ≈ 0 V. Dominant (logic 0): CAN_H ≈ 3.5 V, CAN_L ≈ 1.5 V, differential ≈ 2 V. Receivers decide on the difference: above ~0.9 V is dominant, below ~0.5 V is recessive. Use two probes (or a differential probe) and the math channel H − L. Look for: clean levels, ringing or overshoot on edges (termination), slow edges (capacitance, missing termination), and a dominant ACK bit near the end of each frame (some node received it).
Q: Why twisted pair, and why also run a ground?
Twisting makes both wires pick up nearly the same induced noise (same loop area alternating sign), so it's common-mode and the differential receiver rejects it. It also reduces emissions. The ground keeps both transceivers' common-mode voltages within the allowed range (roughly −2 V to +7 V for standard ISO 11898-2 transceivers). Without it, a ground offset between the boards (from motor currents in the power return, for example) can push the bus outside that range.
Q: Why a 3.3 V transceiver?
The i.MX RT1062 I/O runs at 3.3 V and isn't 5 V tolerant. A transceiver with 5 V logic output on RXD would drive 5 V into an MCU pin and could damage it. Many transceivers have a separate VIO pin to set the logic level; the bus side is still the standard CAN levels.
Q: What's the standby pin and why does it matter?
Many CAN transceivers have a standby or silent pin. In standby the transmitter is off (low power, only wake-up detection). If it floats, it may sit in standby, so the node can't transmit or even ACK. Tie it to the level for normal mode (or drive it from a GPIO).

2.4 CAN fundamentals you should know

  • Arbitration: the bus is wired-AND: dominant (0) overrides recessive (1). Every node transmits its ID bit by bit while listening; if it sends recessive but reads dominant, it lost and backs off. Lowest ID wins, and no data is destroyed ("non-destructive bitwise arbitration"). That's why IDs set priority.
  • Bit stuffing: after 5 identical bits in a row, the transmitter inserts an opposite bit, so receivers get edges to resynchronize to. That's why frame length varies a bit with the data ("+ stuffing" in your equation).
  • ACK: any receiver that got a valid frame drives the ACK slot dominant. A transmitter with no ACK keeps retrying, which is why your transmit is non-blocking with a callback: a missing node won't stall the thread.
  • Error handling: CRC, stuff, form, bit and ACK errors. Each node has transmit and receive error counters. Above 127 it goes error passive; transmit counter above 255 means bus-off (it stops transmitting until recovery). This stops one broken node from jamming the bus.
  • Bit timing: each bit is divided into time quanta: sync, propagation, phase 1, phase 2. The sample point is usually 75–87.5% of the bit. All nodes must agree on bit rate and roughly on sample point.
  • Bus length: during arbitration a bit has to reach the far end and back within one bit time, so at 1 Mbps the bus is limited to roughly 40 m. Propagation is about 5 ns/m.
  • CAN-FD differences: payload up to 64 bytes (lengths 0–8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 48, 64; that's why 43 bytes is padded to 48); the BRS (bit rate switch) bit lets the data phase run faster (your 5 Mbps) while arbitration stays at 1 Mbps so all nodes can arbitrate; stronger CRC (17 or 21 bits) and a stuff-bit counter. At high data rates transceivers need delay compensation, which is one reason you validate on the real harness first.
  • Extended ID: 29 bits. moteus packs a prefix, the source ID, the destination ID and a "reply requested" bit into it. Your hardware filter matches all 29 bits so each controller's replies land in their own queue.

2.5 Frame-time math

One bit at 1 Mbps = 1 µs. A 48-byte CAN-FD frame is about 67 bits in the arbitration phase (start bit, 29-bit ID and control bits, ACK, end of frame, inter-frame space) plus about 430 bits in the data phase (384 data bits + CRC + stuff bits + DLC). At 1 Mbps everywhere: ~500 µs ≈ 0.5 ms. With BRS at 5 Mbps: 67 µs + 430 × 0.2 µs ≈ 153 µs ≈ 0.15 ms.

Q: How did you get 10% bus load?
Four frames per cycle (two commands, two replies) × 0.5 ms = 2 ms per 20 ms = 10%. CAN buses are usually kept under about 50–70% load so lower-priority traffic still gets through and there's room for retransmissions.
Q: Why float32 instead of int16?
moteus can encode registers as int8, int16, int32 or float. The int16 position format has a resolution of 0.0001 rev, so it tops out at ±3.2767 rev. Over ±12° the actuator moves about −8.1 to +7.0 rev, so int16 would overflow. float32 costs a few bytes per register but removes the problem; the frame still fits easily in 48 bytes.

2.6 Firmware ↔ hardware interface (page 2)

Q: What's a devicetree?
A data structure (from Linux, also used by Zephyr) that describes the board's hardware separately from the code: which peripherals exist, which pins they use, clock and bit-rate settings. Zephyr turns it into C macros at build time. My overlay enabled FlexCAN3, set the bit rates (1 Mbps / 5 Mbps), and selected it as the board's CAN bus. Kconfig is the build-time feature switch system (enable CAN, enable CAN-FD mode). I added a compile-time #error if CAN-FD isn't configured, so a wrong build can't reach the bench.
Q: What do hardware acceptance filters do?
The CAN controller compares incoming IDs to filters in hardware and only interrupts the CPU for matches. I add one filter per moteus, matching all 29 bits, and route each to its own message queue. That keeps unrelated traffic away from the CPU and means the pitch reply can never be confused with the yaw reply.
Q: What if the bus is stuck or a node is unplugged?
Transmit is non-blocking: can_send with K_NO_WAIT and a completion callback, so the frame goes into a hardware mailbox and the thread continues. If nobody ACKs, the controller keeps retrying in hardware, but my thread isn't waiting on it. The real liveness check is the reply: no reply for 3 cycles = fault. Then the moteus's own 100 ms watchdog stops the motor if commands stop.

2.8 The kinematics

Picture a triangle with corners at the gimbal pivot, the actuator's mount on the structure, and the actuator's mount on the engine. Two sides are fixed lengths (pivot to structure mount, Lts; pivot to engine mount, Lre). The third side is the actuator, L. The angle between the fixed sides is θ₀ at center, and the gimbal angle δ adds to it. Law of cosines:

L² = L_ts² + L_re² − 2·L_ts·L_re·cos(θ₀ + δ)

  • Inverse direction (length → angle, for telemetry and fault checks): cos(θ₀+δ) = (L_ts² + L_re² − L²) / (2·L_ts·L_re), then δ = acos(…) − θ₀. The code clamps the acos argument to [−1, 1] so a noisy measurement can't produce NaN.
  • Why asymmetric travel (−8.1 vs +7.0 rev): L depends on cos(θ₀+δ), which isn't linear in δ, so equal angle steps give unequal length steps.
  • Sensitivity: dL/dδ ≤ 0.115 in/deg over ±12°. I use the maximum to convert an angle-rate limit into an actuator-speed limit conservatively: 30°/s × 0.115 ≈ 3.45 in/s ≈ 22 motor rev/s.
  • Validation: unit tests compare the firmware to 241 points per axis (−12° to +12° in 0.1° steps) generated from the MATLAB model, plus tests that length → angle inverts angle → length and that length increases monotonically with angle.
  • Precomputed: L_ts² + L_re² and 2·L_ts·L_re are constants computed at compile time.
Q: Why closed form instead of a lookup table?
It's exact, tiny (a sqrt and a cos), invertible analytically, and there's no table to keep in sync with the geometry. A Cortex-M7 with a hardware FPU does it in well under a microsecond. A lookup table would make sense if the geometry had no closed-form solution.

2.9 Lead screw and motor physics (likely if they're electrical-leaning)

  • Lead = linear travel per revolution = 4 mm (single-start, so lead = pitch).
  • Force from torque: F = 2π·η·T / lead. Example with T = 1 N·m, lead = 0.004 m and efficiency η ≈ 0.3 (typical ACME lead screw): F ≈ 2π × 0.3 × 1 / 0.004 ≈ 470 N (about 105 lbf). Ball screws are ~0.9 efficient. Find out which type your actuator uses.
  • Backdriving: low-efficiency lead screws resist being pushed back by the load; ball screws backdrive easily. Matters for what the gimbal does when the motor is unpowered.
  • Torque ∝ current in a BLDC: T = Kt · I. So "torque limit" really means "phase current limit".
  • Back-EMF: a spinning motor generates voltage ∝ speed (Ke). At high speed it limits how fast you can go for a given supply voltage.
  • Regeneration: when the load pushes the motor (decelerating, or the engine pushing back), the motor acts as a generator and can pump current back into the supply, raising the bus voltage. Supplies need capacitance or a brake resistor to absorb it. Good thing to mention if they ask about actuator power.
  • FOC (field-oriented control): the controller measures phase currents and rotor angle and controls the current vector so it's always 90° to the rotor's magnetic field, which gives maximum torque per amp and smooth torque.

2.10 Safety supervisor (page 3)

Q: Why do you need homing?
The moteus encoder measures motor angle, but after power-up it doesn't know where gimbal center is in absolute terms across multiple revolutions. Homing defines the current position as zero while the gimbal is mechanically centered. The supervisor refuses to enable until moteus reports it has been homed since power-up AND the measured length is within 0.05 in of center. A Teensy reboot alone doesn't need re-homing as long as the moteus stayed powered and is still near center.
Q: Why latch faults instead of auto-recovering?
A fault means something unexpected happened. Auto-recovering could hide a real problem and let the system oscillate between working and failing. Latching forces a person to look, then re-home, which also re-establishes the position reference.
Q: Why 3 missed cycles, and why 5 cycles for tracking error?
One missed reply can be a transient (a corrupted frame that got retried late). Three in a row (60 ms) is a real loss, and it's still faster than the moteus' 100 ms watchdog, so the flight computer notices first. The tracking-error persistence filter (5 cycles = 100 ms) keeps normal lag during a fast move from tripping it. Both are tunable, and the tracking limit (0.10 in) is marked to tune from measured response.
Q: "Wrong mode" fault?
If the moteus isn't in position mode while we think it's enabled (it reset, faulted, or timed out), it's not following commands. It gets a 3-cycle grace period, then it faults.
Likely hard question: "Is centering the gimbal actually safe in flight?" Good answer: "On the test stand, yes: centering points thrust straight down the stand's axis, and the test aborts. In flight it's a vehicle-level decision. Centering a gimbal mid-flight could be worse than holding the last angle, so the right response depends on flight phase, and the real answer might be engine shutdown or a redundant actuator. My supervisor makes the policy configurable (hold center or stop), and the abort is signaled to the controller, so GNC can own the flight decision." This shows you understand fail-safe vs fail-operational.

2.11 Real-time design (page 4)

Q: Explain absolute-period scheduling vs sleeping.
while(1) { work(); sleep(20ms); } has a period of 20 ms + work time + scheduling delay, so it drifts and the frequency isn't exactly 50 Hz. A periodic kernel timer fires every 20 ms regardless of how long the work took; the thread blocks on the timer status (k_timer_status_sync), which also returns how many periods expired. More than 1 means you missed a cycle, which counts as an overrun instead of silently stretching the period.
Q: Explain your thread priorities.
In Zephyr a lower number is higher priority, and negative priorities are cooperative: they aren't preempted by other threads, only by interrupts, until they block or yield. The controller work queue is −10 (highest), TVC is −7, sensor threads are −5. So when both are ready, the controller tick always runs first, and a slow sensor read can never delay a TVC cycle. Because TVC spends almost all its time blocked on the timer or reply queues, its CPU use is tiny.
Q: What's priority inversion and how did you avoid it?
A high-priority thread waits on a mutex held by a low-priority thread, and a medium-priority thread preempts the low one, so the high one waits indefinitely (the classic Mars Pathfinder bug). Zephyr mutexes do priority inheritance (the holder is temporarily boosted), but I avoided it by design: the shared data is one small struct, both sides only copy it while holding the lock, and nothing blocks, sends on CAN or logs inside the lock. I moved a log call out of the critical section because immediate-mode logging could stall the controller.
Q: What's jitter and how do you measure it?
Jitter is variation in the actual period around the nominal 20 ms. Each cycle I timestamp the wake-up with the CPU cycle counter, compute the period, and track the max deviation over a 1 s window. Also tracked: per-axis reply latency, max cycle time, overruns, missed replies and rejected transmits, logged every 10 s and sent in telemetry.
Be ready: "What jitter did you actually measure?" If you have a real number from the bench, say it. If you don't, say: "The instrumentation reports it every cycle; I don't have a number I'd quote with confidence right now. The design budget is that both replies come back within the 5 ms window, and overruns are counted, not hidden." Never invent a number.

2.12 The two problems (page 4)

Static initialization order: explain it like I'm an EE.
In C++, global objects in different source files are constructed before main(), but the order between files isn't defined. A geometry constant (2·L_ts·L_re) was a global computed at startup. The supervisor was also a global whose constructor used that constant to precompute the length limits. If the supervisor's file happened to initialize first, the constant was still 0, the min and max lengths collapsed to the same value, and every command would be clamped to one length. Unit tests passed because there the order happened to be right. Fix: make the constant constexpr, computed by the compiler (I wrote a compile-time Newton's-method square root because std::sqrt isn't constexpr), plus a static_assert that its value is in the expected range. Now it can't be wrong at runtime, and a geometry change that breaks it fails the build.
The threading problem: what was racing?
Waiting for CAN replies (up to 5 ms) inside the 1.5 ms controller tick would stall every control task. Moving CAN to its own thread created shared state (latest command in, latest status out) touched by two threads. The fix: one thread owns the bus; the hand-off is a single struct behind a mutex, copied in and out; nothing slow happens under the lock. The NaN fix: the bench log converted a float to an integer for printing, and converting NaN (e.g. a value that wasn't available that cycle) to an int is undefined behavior in C++, so I check for NaN first. Know which specific symptom you saw; if they ask "how did you notice the race?", answer from your memory, not this guide.

3.2 Components

  • Diode: conducts one way, ~0.7 V drop (silicon), ~0.3 V (Schottky). Uses: reverse-polarity protection, rectification, flyback.
  • Flyback diode: across an inductive load (solenoid, relay coil, motor). When the switch opens, the inductor's current continues through the diode instead of creating a huge voltage spike that kills the switch. Downside: slow turn-off. A Zener or TVS clamp turns off faster.
  • TVS diode: clamps transient overvoltage (ESD, load dump, spikes) by conducting hard above its breakdown voltage. Put them on connector pins that leave the box, like CAN lines.
  • MOSFET as a switch: N-channel low-side switch (between load and ground) is simplest: gate high = on. Key specs: RDS(on) (conduction loss I²R), VGS(th) (need enough gate voltage to fully turn on, not just the threshold), VDS rating, gate charge (switching speed). High-side switching (between supply and load) usually uses P-channel or an N-channel with a gate driver/charge pump. High-side is preferred when you want the load grounded when off.
  • BJT vs MOSFET: BJT is current-controlled (base current), MOSFET is voltage-controlled (gate is a capacitor, ~zero DC current). MOSFETs dominate power switching.
  • Op-amp: ideal rules: no current into the inputs; with negative feedback, the inputs sit at the same voltage. Non-inverting gain = 1 + Rf/Rg; inverting gain = −Rf/Rin. Uses: amplifying sensor signals, buffering, filtering, differential/instrumentation amps.
  • Pull-up/pull-down resistors: set a defined default level on an input that would otherwise float (like your transceiver standby pin). Required on open-drain buses like I²C.
  • Decoupling capacitors: small ceramic caps (e.g. 100 nF) right at each IC power pin supply fast current spikes locally, so the switching current doesn't travel through the power trace inductance. Plus bulk caps for slower demand.

3.3 Power

  • Linear regulator (LDO): simple and quiet, but wastes (Vin − Vout) × I as heat. 12 V → 3.3 V at 1 A wastes 8.7 W. Efficiency ≈ Vout/Vin.
  • Switching converters: buck (step down), boost (step up), buck-boost. 85–95% efficient, but produce switching noise, so they need filtering and layout care.
  • Inrush current: big input capacitors look like a short at power-on. Limit it with a soft-start, NTC thermistor, or hot-swap controller.
  • Protection: fuses (one-time) or resettable PTCs/eFuses for overcurrent; a series diode or ideal-diode MOSFET for reverse polarity; TVS for transients; undervoltage lockout so electronics don't brown out unpredictably.
  • Power budget: list every load's voltage, nominal and peak current, and duty cycle; sum per rail; add margin (commonly 20–50%); check battery capacity (Ah) and wire/connector ratings against peaks. Motor stall current is the peak to design for.
  • Batteries: capacity in Ah, energy in Wh = V × Ah. Li-ion cell ~3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V full, ~3.0 V empty. C-rate: 1C draws the full capacity in 1 hour.
Know your actuator power before 2:00 They may ask "how are the actuators powered?" Find out the supply voltage, what battery or supply, the moteus input current at stall, and whether it's fused. If you don't know: "That's owned by our electrical lead; the moteus reports bus voltage in my telemetry, and here's how I'd size the supply..." (then walk through a power budget). Mention regen: a backdriven motor pumps energy back into the bus.

3.4 Wire harnesses and connectors (core electrical systems topic)

  • Wire sizing: pick gauge for (1) current without overheating (with derating for bundles, altitude and temperature: wires in a bundle can carry less than in free air), and (2) acceptable voltage drop: ΔV = I × Rwire × length (both directions!). Rough numbers: 22 AWG ≈ 53 mΩ/m, 20 AWG ≈ 33 mΩ/m, 18 AWG ≈ 21 mΩ/m, 16 AWG ≈ 13 mΩ/m. Every 3 AWG steps is about 2× the area.
  • Worked example: 10 A over 2 m of 18 AWG (out and back = 4 m): R ≈ 4 × 0.021 = 0.084 Ω; ΔV ≈ 0.84 V; loss ≈ 8.4 W. On a 24 V bus that's 3.5% drop. Fine for a motor, maybe not for a sensor reference.
  • Twisting: twist signal pairs and power/return pairs together. That minimizes the loop area, which reduces both emitted and picked-up magnetic noise.
  • Shielding: a braided or foil shield blocks electric-field coupling. Low-frequency rule: ground the shield at one end to avoid ground loops. At high frequency, ground at both ends (360° at the connector backshell). Many aerospace harnesses do both ends.
  • Connectors: aerospace uses circular MIL-spec (e.g. MIL-DTL-38999) with locking, keying, sealing and backshells. Derate pin current, use keying so mating connectors can't be swapped, and don't put power and sensitive signals on adjacent pins.
  • Crimp vs solder: crimps are preferred in flight hardware: solder wicks into the strands and makes a stiff point that cracks under vibration.
  • Strain relief, service loops, bend radius, labeling, and keeping the harness away from heat and sharp edges.
  • Testing a harness: continuity (each pin goes where the drawing says), isolation/insulation resistance (no shorts between pins or to shield, often a "hipot" or megger test at high voltage), and checking against the wiring diagram/pinout (the ICD).

3.5 Grounding and EMI

  • Ground loop: two grounded points at slightly different potentials connected by more than one path. Current flows in the loop and shows up as noise on signals that use ground as reference. Fix: single-point grounding, differential signals, isolation.
  • Return current: every current returns to its source. At high frequency the return follows the path of least inductance, right under the signal trace. Breaking the return path (split ground plane) creates big loops that radiate.
  • Separate noisy and quiet: keep motor/power currents from flowing through the ground reference of sensitive analog circuits. Star ground or separate returns that join at one point.
  • Differential signaling (CAN, RS-422, LVDS, Ethernet) rejects common-mode noise; that's your page-2 argument.
  • Coupling types: capacitive (electric field, high dV/dt; fix with distance or shielding), inductive (magnetic, high dI/dt; fix by reducing loop area and twisting), conducted (through shared power or ground; fix with filtering).

3.6 Sensors and signal acquisition

  • ADC: resolution N bits → step (LSB) = Vref/2N. 12-bit at 3.3 V ≈ 0.8 mV. SAR ADCs are common in microcontrollers; sigma-delta for high resolution at low speed.
  • Nyquist: sample at more than twice the highest frequency in the signal, or higher frequencies alias (fold) into the band you care about. Put an analog low-pass (anti-aliasing) filter before the ADC.
  • Pressure transducers: often 0.5–4.5 V ratiometric or 4–20 mA. A 4–20 mA current loop ignores wire resistance and voltage drop, and 0 mA means a broken wire, so you can detect the fault.
  • Thermocouples: two metals produce a small voltage (~40 µV/°C for type K) proportional to the temperature difference between the hot junction and where the wires terminate, so you need cold-junction compensation. Tiny signal, so amplify and filter.
  • Strain gauge / Wheatstone bridge: four resistors; the gauge's tiny resistance change unbalances the bridge, giving a small differential voltage you amplify with an instrumentation amp. Used in load cells (thrust measurement).
  • Encoders: quadrature (A/B channels 90° apart, gives direction) or absolute. The moteus uses a magnetic encoder on the motor.

4. Embedded software and RTOS concepts

  • RTOS vs bare metal: an RTOS gives threads with priorities, preemption, timers, and synchronization (mutexes, semaphores, queues), with predictable timing. Bare metal is a super-loop plus interrupts.
  • Deterministic means the worst-case timing is bounded and known, not just the average. Avoid unbounded loops, dynamic memory, blocking calls with no timeout, and logging in hot paths.
  • Mutex vs semaphore: a mutex is ownership (lock/unlock by the same thread, supports priority inheritance); a semaphore is a counter used for signaling between threads or ISRs. You used a mutex for shared state and a timer/message queue for signaling.
  • Message queue: thread-safe FIFO; your CAN receive callback (ISR context) puts frames in a queue, and the thread takes them with a timeout. That's how you wait "up to 5 ms" for replies.
  • ISRs: keep them short; no blocking. Hand work to a thread.
  • Why allocation-free: malloc/new in real time is non-deterministic (variable time) and fragments memory over time. Your moteus encoder/decoder uses fixed 64-byte buffers, no heap.
  • volatile vs atomic: volatile stops the compiler from caching a variable (for hardware registers), but doesn't make read-modify-write safe between threads. Use atomics or a mutex for that. Your transmit-error counters are atomics because the ISR callback increments them.
  • Undefined behavior: e.g. converting NaN or an out-of-range float to an int. The compiler may do anything. Check before converting.
  • std::expected: C++23 type holding either a value or an error, used instead of exceptions (often disabled on embedded).
  • Watchdog timers: hardware that resets the system (or, for the moteus, stops the motor) if software stops "petting" it. Catches hangs.
  • Unit tests on native_sim: Zephyr can build for the host PC, so hardware-independent logic runs as a normal program. That's why you kept the supervisor, kinematics and protocol free of CAN/driver dependencies.

5. Controls, briefly

  • PID: P reacts to the current error, I removes steady-state error (but can wind up while saturated), D damps by reacting to the rate of change (but amplifies noise, so it's usually filtered). Anti-windup: stop integrating when the output is saturated.
  • Cascaded loops: inner loop much faster than the outer loop (rule of thumb 5–10×). Yours: moteus current → velocity → position at kHz rates, inside your 50 Hz setpoint loop, inside GNC.
  • Latency hurts stability: a delay adds phase lag that grows with frequency and eats phase margin. That's why you keep reply latency under 5 ms and bounded.
  • Saturation and rate limits are nonlinearities: the controller has to know about them or they cause windup and overshoot.
  • TVC physics: pitching moment = T · d · sin δ, where T is thrust, d is the distance from the gimbal to the vehicle's center of mass, and δ is the gimbal angle. Lateral force = T sin δ; you lose T(1 − cos δ) of axial thrust (about 2% at 12°).

6. Systems engineering and safety

  • Requirements: verifiable ("shall respond within 5 ms"), not vague ("shall be fast"). Each has a verification method (TAID) and traces up to a higher-level need and down to design. You did this on a small scale with R1–R5.
  • Interface control document (ICD): defines every signal between subsystems: pinout, voltage levels, protocol, timing. Your page-2 interface table is a mini ICD.
  • FMEA (failure modes and effects analysis): for each component, list how it can fail, the effect, how you'd detect it, and the mitigation. Example for your system: CAN wire breaks → no replies → detected in 60 ms → FAULT, gimbal to center, abort; moteus watchdog stops the motor at 100 ms.
  • Single points of failure: in your design, the CAN bus is shared by both actuators, so a short on CAN_H/CAN_L takes out both axes. A flight design might use separate buses or a redundant bus. Saying this unprompted shows maturity.
  • Fail-safe vs fail-operational: fail-safe = go to a safe state (stop) when something breaks; fine for a test stand. Fail-operational = keep working through a failure (needs redundancy); often required for crewed vehicles in critical phases.
  • Test like you fly: test with flight harnesses, flight software builds and realistic conditions. Your "verify signal quality on the real vehicle harness before raising the bit rate" is an example.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL): real flight computer and/or actuators connected to a simulation of the rest of the vehicle. Your next step.
